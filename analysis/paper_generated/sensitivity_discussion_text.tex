Full organization produces lower mean mortality than no organization at every tested parameter value. Each comparison's Monte Carlo interval also lies below zero. With a depth-reduction coefficient of 0.25, the mean mortality change from no to full organization is -4.32 [-4.64, -4.00] percentage points, changing by +3.53 [+3.29, +3.78] percentage points from the primary difference. This positive shift means a smaller modeled mortality reduction. The findings for full organization do not establish a consistent benefit from social-capital increases alone, or prove that social capital has no effect.

At 30\% shelter capacity, mean mortality is higher at the far site than at the near site at every tested parameter value. Each interval for the far-minus-near difference lies above zero, although the size of the difference changes with people per trip and hospital capacity. At three people per boat trip, increasing the far-site fleet from one to five boats changes mean mortality by -0.63 [-0.82, -0.45] percentage points; its change from the primary difference has Holm-adjusted $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.0374$. At nine people per trip, the mortality change is -0.03 [-0.23, +0.17] percentage points. Its interval spans zero, so this setting does not clearly establish a mortality benefit from the additional boats. Nor does it show that additional boats cause harm. Some parameter changes alter the fleet comparison after adjustment; the primary result is therefore not a general estimate of the benefit of adding boats. 

Hospital-capacity results depend on the common hourly injury-survival deadline and the fixed recovery-eligibility rule. An injury continues to progress outside hospital, including while waiting in shelter and after the flood. Evacuees are assumed to receive off-site care under the same recovery rule without using local beds; this access assumption is not empirically calibrated. Boundary tests verify identical survival deadlines for nonrecovering people across care states. Applying the same rule to every injured person does not guarantee that adding beds lowers total mortality in every random run: admissions, discharge and later exposure can also change. Recovery assumptions, survival deadlines and off-site access need independent evidence before the results can guide clinical planning.

The study tests selected comparisons under selected assumptions; it does not fit the parameters or establish that the findings hold under every plausible model. A change in the difference between settings shows sensitivity to a parameter, not which intermediate process caused the change. Mortality can rise similarly in both settings while their difference changes little. Hospital-capacity tests retain the same injury and discharge rules. Boat-capacity tests change people per trip, not loading time, travel speed or routing. Alternative depth dynamics, survival rules, household construction and return or discharge processes are not tested here. Changing several assumptions together may also produce responses not captured by these individual tests.
